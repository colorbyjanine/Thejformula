 1                               INTRODUCTION
 2  
 3       Plaintiff Dr. Elaine Whitcombe respectfully submits this Memorandum of
 4  Points and Authorities in Opposition to Defendant Kingswell Preparatory
 5  Academy's ("KPA") Motion for Summary Judgment. KPA seeks summary judgment on
 6  Dr. Whitcombe's claim for harassment based on age in violation of the
 7  California Fair Employment and Housing Act ("FEHA"), Government Code section
 8  12940, subdivision (j).
 9  
10       The motion should be denied. A triable issue of material fact exists as
11  to whether Defendant's supervisor, Richard Langford, subjected Dr. Whitcombe
12  to harassment sufficiently severe to create a hostile work environment.
13  California law is clear that a single incident of harassment can be
14  sufficiently severe to constitute actionable harassment, and that harassment
15  cases are "rarely appropriate" for summary judgment. Gov. Code § 12923(b),
16  (e). Here, Mr. Langford's demeaning, age-based comments—made publicly in
17  front of Dr. Whitcombe's students—were severe enough that a reasonable jury
18  could find they altered the conditions of her employment and created a
19  hostile work environment.

25  Academy, a private high school in San Diego.

11                              LEGAL STANDARD
12  
13       Summary judgment is proper only when "all the papers submitted show that
14  there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party
15  is entitled to judgment as a matter of law." Code Civ. Proc. § 437c(c). The
16  moving party bears the initial burden of showing that a cause of action has
17  no merit by demonstrating "that one or more elements of the cause of
18  action . . . cannot be established, or that there is a complete defense to
19  the cause of action." Code Civ. Proc. § 437c(p)(2).
20  
21       In ruling on a motion for summary judgment, the court must view the
22  evidence in the light most favorable to the opposing party and resolve all
23  doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party. Aguilar v. Atlantic
24  Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843. The court does not weigh the
25  evidence or assess credibility; those determinations are reserved for the
26  trier of fact. Id. at 856.
27  
28       Summary judgment is a "drastic remedy" that should be used "with caution


PLAINTIFF'S OPPOSITION TO DEFENDANT'S MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT
                                    - 5 -

 1  so that it does not become a substitute for the open trial method of
 2  determining facts." Molko v. Holy Spirit Ass'n (1988) 46 Cal.3d 1092, 1107.
 3  The opposing party need only demonstrate that a triable issue of material
 4  fact exists—not that they would prevail at trial. Code Civ. Proc.
 5  § 437c(p)(2).

 7                                 ARGUMENT
 8  
 9       A. A Single Incident of Age-Based Harassment by a Supervisor Can
10         Create a Triable Issue of Fact Under FEHA When the Conduct Is
11         Sufficiently Severe
12  
13       California law expressly provides that "[a] single incident of harassing
14  conduct is sufficient to create a triable issue regarding the existence of a
15  hostile work environment if the harassing conduct has unreasonably interfered
16  with the plaintiff's work performance or created an intimidating, hostile, or
17  offensive working environment." Gov. Code § 12923(b).
18  
19       The California Supreme Court recently reaffirmed this principle in
20  Bailey v. San Francisco District Attorney's Office (2024) 16 Cal.5th 611. In
21  Bailey, the Court held that a single use of a racial epithet by a coworker
22  could be sufficiently severe to constitute actionable harassment under FEHA.
23  The Court emphasized that courts must consider "the totality of the
24  circumstances," including "the specific word or words used, the speaker,
25  whether it was directed at the plaintiff, and the larger social context of
26  the workplace." Id. at 631.
27  
28       The Court in Bailey reversed summary judgment, holding that there was a


PLAINTIFF'S OPPOSITION TO DEFENDANT'S MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT
                                    - 6 -

 1  triable issue of fact as to whether the single incident was actionable
 2  harassment. Critically, the Court recognized that certain conduct—even in
 3  isolation—can be so severe as to alter the conditions of employment. Id. at
 4  630-32.
 5  
 6       While Bailey involved racial harassment, the same principles apply with
 7  equal force to age-based harassment. Indeed, courts have applied Government
 8  Code section 12923(b) to age harassment claims. See Milner v. TBWA Worldwide,
 9  Inc. (C.D. Cal. 2019) 2019 WL 5617757, at *4 ("Under California law, even one
10  instance of harassment can be sufficient" to establish a FEHA age-based
11  harassment claim).
12  
13       Here, Mr. Langford's conduct was severe. He publicly mocked Dr.
14  Whitcombe in front of her students, comparing her to a dinosaur and declaring
15  that she could not learn new technology because she was an "old dog." These
16  comments directly attacked her competence based solely on her age. They were
17  made by her supervisor—an assistant principal with direct authority over her
18  employment. And they were made in the most damaging context possible: in
19  front of the very students she was trying to educate and command respect
20  from.
21  
22       The severity is further demonstrated by the reaction in the room—
23  students gasped, no one laughed, and Dr. Whitcombe was reduced to standing in
24  shocked silence before fleeing to cry in the bathroom. This is precisely the
25  type of humiliating, degrading conduct that the Legislature sought to address
26  when it codified the single-incident rule in section 12923(b).
27

 1       B. The Totality of the Circumstances Demonstrates That Mr. Langford's
 2         Conduct Was Severe Enough to Create a Hostile Work Environment
 3  
 4       Government Code section 12923(c) requires courts to consider harassment
 5  claims "under a totality of the circumstances" analysis. The Legislature
 6  specifically rejected narrow interpretations that would dismiss offensive
 7  remarks as "stray" comments. See Gov. Code § 12923(c) ("[T]he Legislature
 8  affirms its rejection of the 'stray remarks doctrine'").
 9  
10       Under the totality of the circumstances, the following factors establish
11  that Mr. Langford's harassment was severe:
12  
13       1. The Comments Were Made by a Supervisor
14  
15       Mr. Langford was not a mere coworker. He was Dr. Whitcombe's direct
16  supervisor—an assistant principal with authority over teachers, including the
17  power to affect their employment. Under FEHA, an employer is strictly liable
18  for harassment by a supervisor. Gov. Code § 12940(j)(1); see also State Dep't
19  of Health Servs. v. Superior Court (2003) 31 Cal.4th 1026, 1042.
20  
21       The Bailey Court emphasized that even a coworker's single comment can be
22  actionable, while noting that a "supervisor's words may carry more weight and
23  be more intimidating." Bailey, 16 Cal.5th at 631. When a supervisor publicly
24  demeans an employee, the impact is magnified—the employee reasonably fears
25  that the supervisor's hostility will translate into adverse employment
26  consequences.
27  
28       2. The Comments Were Made Publicly in Front of Students

                                    - 8 -

 1       Mr. Langford did not make his comments privately. He humiliated Dr.
 2  Whitcombe in front of her students—the very individuals she needed to respect
 3  her authority as their teacher. This public degradation fundamentally
 4  undermined Dr. Whitcombe's ability to perform her job.
 5  
 6       As the undisputed facts show, after the incident, Dr. Whitcombe's
 7  students would not look her in the eye. For the first time in her career, she
 8  felt unable to establish authority in her classroom. This is precisely the
 9  kind of interference with work performance that FEHA is designed to prevent.
10  See Gov. Code § 12923(a) (harassment must be evaluated by whether "a
11  reasonable person subjected to the discriminatory conduct would find . . .
12  that the harassment so altered working conditions as to make it more
13  difficult to do the job").
14  
15       3. The Content of the Comments Was Directly Age-Based and Degrading
16  
17       Mr. Langford's comments were not ambiguous. He compared Dr. Whitcombe to
18  a prehistoric creature ("dinosaurs"), mocked her ability to learn ("You can't
19  teach an old dog new tricks"), and directly challenged her professional
20  competence based on her age ("I don't think you're going to be able to figure
21  out the new LMS").
22  
23       These are classic age-based stereotypes—that older workers are obsolete,
24  incapable of learning, and should be put out to pasture. Such stereotypes are
25  exactly what FEHA prohibits. See Gov. Code § 12940(j)(1) (prohibiting
26  harassment based on age).
27  
28       4. The Harm Was Severe and Documented

                                    - 9 -

 1       The severity of the harassment is demonstrated by its devastating
 2  effects on Dr. Whitcombe. She immediately fled the classroom in tears. She
 3  developed severe stress and anxiety. She was forced to take leave. And she
 4  has been unable to return to work.
 5  
 6       Dr. Samuel Pierre, a psychiatrist, has determined that Dr. Whitcombe
 7  suffered a permanent disability as a result of the August 26th incident. This
 8  documented harm demonstrates that the harassment was not trivial or
 9  fleeting—it fundamentally altered the conditions of Dr. Whitcombe's
10  employment and destroyed her ability to perform her job.
11  
12       C. Harassment Cases Are Rarely Appropriate for Summary Judgment, and
13         This Case Presents Triable Issues of Fact
14  
15       The California Legislature has expressly declared that "[h]arassment
16  cases are rarely appropriate for disposition on summary judgment." Gov. Code
17  § 12923(e). The Legislature recognized that hostile work environment cases
18  "involve issues 'not determinable on paper.'" Id.
19  
20       This legislative directive exists because determinations about the
21  severity of harassment depend heavily on context, credibility, and the trier
22  of fact's assessment of the circumstances. Whether Mr. Langford's comments
23  were severe enough to create a hostile work environment is precisely the type
24  of factual question that a jury—not a court on summary judgment—should
25  decide.
26  
27       Here, there are triable issues of fact concerning:

 1       • The severity of Mr. Langford's conduct in context;
 2  
 3       • The impact of the public humiliation on Dr. Whitcombe's ability to do

 5  
 6       • Whether a reasonable person would find the conduct created a hostile
 7         environment; and
 8  
 9       • The extent of the harm Dr. Whitcombe suffered.
10  
11       KPA may argue that one incident is insufficient as a matter of law, but
12  that argument is foreclosed by Government Code section 12923(b) and Bailey.
13  The question is not whether a single incident can be actionable—the
14  Legislature and Supreme Court have confirmed it can—but whether this incident
15  was sufficiently severe. That is a factual determination for a jury.

23  FEHA. This case should proceed to trial.
