## I. INTRODUCTION

Plaintiff Dr. Elaine Whitcombe respectfully submits this Memorandum of Points and Authorities in Opposition to Defendant Kingswell Preparatory Academy's ("KPA") Motion for Summary Judgment. KPA seeks summary judgment on Dr. Whitcombe's claim for harassment based on age in violation of the California Fair Employment and Housing Act ("FEHA"), Government Code section 12940, subdivision (j).

The motion should be denied. A triable issue of material fact exists as to whether Defendant's supervisor, Richard Langford, subjected Dr. Whitcombe to harassment sufficiently severe to create a hostile work environment. California law is clear that a single incident of harassment can be sufficiently severe to constitute actionable harassment, and that harassment cases are "rarely appropriate" for summary judgment. Gov. Code § 12923(b), (e). Here, Mr. Langford's demeaning, age-based comments—made publicly in front of Dr. Whitcombe's students—were severe enough that a reasonable jury could find they altered the conditions of her employment and created a hostile work environment.

---

## II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

Dr. Elaine Whitcombe is a 60-year-old high school English teacher who has dedicated her career to educating students at Kingswell Preparatory Academy, a private high school in San Diego. (Facts ¶ 1.)

In June 2024, KPA's principal, Headmaster Shari Wainwright, informed all teachers that the school would be implementing new teaching pedagogy and technology, including AI-resistant assessments. (Facts ¶ 3.) Dr. Whitcombe did not feel harassed by these requirements or the subsequent training. (Facts ¶ 4.)

In July 2024, KPA hired Richard Langford as assistant principal. (Facts ¶ 5.) Mr. Langford had supervisory authority over teachers, including Dr. Whitcombe, and was tasked with overseeing implementation of the new teaching methods and technologies. (Id.)

On August 26, 2024—the first day of school—Mr. Langford observed Dr. Whitcombe's class. (Facts ¶ 6.) During the class, Dr. Whitcombe made a self-deprecating joke to her students about technology, stating: "I know I may look like I've been around since the dark ages, but trust me, I'm very versed in current technology and will be able to tell if you are plagiarizing." (Facts ¶ 7.) The students laughed. (Id.)

Dr. Whitcombe then mentioned that the class would be using a new learning management system and that it "might take all of us a few weeks to become proficient." (Facts ¶ 8.)

Mr. Langford then interjected with the following comments directed at Dr. Whitcombe: "The dark ages? You look more like you've been around since dinosaurs walked the earth. I don't think you're going to be able to figure out the new LMS. You can't teach an old dog new tricks." (Facts ¶ 9.)

No one laughed. Two students gasped. (Facts ¶ 10.) Dr. Whitcombe stood in shock, humiliated, and unsure what to do. (Id.) After approximately two minutes of standing in silence, Dr. Whitcombe walked out of her classroom, went to the teachers' bathroom, and cried. (Facts ¶ 11.) She left work early that day. (Id.)

The impact was devastating and lasting. The next day, and the day after, none of Dr. Whitcombe's students would look her in the eye. (Facts ¶ 12.) For the first time in her teaching career, she felt unable to establish her authority in the classroom. (Id.) She began suffering from severe stress and anxiety and had to take leave. (Facts ¶ 13.)

Dr. Samuel Pierre, Plaintiff's expert psychiatrist, determined that Dr. Whitcombe suffered from a permanent disability due to the stress from the August 26th incident. (Facts ¶ 13.) She has been unable to return to work. (Id.)

## III. LEGAL STANDARD

Summary judgment is proper only when "all the papers submitted show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law." Code Civ. Proc. § 437c(c). The moving party bears the initial burden of showing that a cause of action has no merit by demonstrating "that one or more elements of the cause of action . . . cannot be established, or that there is a complete defense to the cause of action." Code Civ. Proc. § 437c(p)(2).

In ruling on a motion for summary judgment, the court must view the evidence in the light most favorable to the opposing party and resolve all doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party. Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843. The court does not weigh the evidence or assess credibility; those determinations are reserved for the trier of fact. Id. at 856.

Summary judgment is a "drastic remedy" that should be used "with caution so that it does not become a substitute for the open trial method of determining facts." Molko v. Holy Spirit Ass'n (1988) 46 Cal.3d 1092, 1107. The opposing party need only demonstrate that a triable issue of material fact exists—not that they would prevail at trial. Code Civ. Proc. § 437c(p)(2).

## IV. ARGUMENT

**A. A Single Incident of Age-Based Harassment by a Supervisor Can Create a Triable Issue of Fact Under FEHA When the Conduct Is Sufficiently Severe**

California law expressly provides that "[a] single incident of harassing conduct is sufficient to create a triable issue regarding the existence of a hostile work environment if the harassing conduct has unreasonably interfered with the plaintiff's work performance or created an intimidating, hostile, or offensive working environment." Gov. Code § 12923(b).

The California Supreme Court recently reaffirmed this principle in Bailey v. San Francisco District Attorney's Office (2024) 16 Cal.5th 611. In Bailey, the Court held that a single use of a racial epithet by a coworker could be sufficiently severe to constitute actionable harassment under FEHA. The Court emphasized that courts must consider "the totality of the circumstances," including "the specific word or words used, the speaker, whether it was directed at the plaintiff, and the larger social context of the workplace." Id. at 631.

The Court in Bailey reversed summary judgment, holding that there was a triable issue of fact as to whether the single incident was actionable harassment. Critically, the Court recognized that certain conduct—even in isolation—can be so severe as to alter the conditions of employment. Id. at 630-32.

While Bailey involved racial harassment, the same principles apply with equal force to age-based harassment. Indeed, courts have applied Government Code section 12923(b) to age harassment claims. See Milner v. TBWA Worldwide, Inc. (C.D. Cal. 2019) 2019 WL 5617757, at *4 ("Under California law, even one instance of harassment can be sufficient" to establish a FEHA age-based harassment claim).

Here, Mr. Langford's conduct was severe. He publicly mocked Dr. Whitcombe in front of her students, comparing her to a dinosaur and declaring that she could not learn new technology because she was an "old dog." These comments directly attacked her competence based solely on her age. They were made by her supervisor—an assistant principal with direct authority over her employment. And they were made in the most damaging context possible: in front of the very students she was trying to educate and command respect from.

The severity is further demonstrated by the reaction in the room—students gasped, no one laughed, and Dr. Whitcombe was reduced to standing in shocked silence before fleeing to cry in the bathroom. This is precisely the type of humiliating, degrading conduct that the Legislature sought to address when it codified the single-incident rule in section 12923(b).

**B. The Totality of the Circumstances Demonstrates That Mr. Langford's Conduct Was Severe Enough to Create a Hostile Work Environment**

Government Code section 12923(c) requires courts to consider harassment claims "under a totality of the circumstances" analysis. The Legislature specifically rejected narrow interpretations that would dismiss offensive remarks as "stray" comments. See Gov. Code § 12923(c) ("[T]he Legislature affirms its rejection of the 'stray remarks doctrine'").

Under the totality of the circumstances, the following factors establish that Mr. Langford's harassment was severe:

*1. The Comments Were Made by a Supervisor*

Mr. Langford was not a mere coworker. He was Dr. Whitcombe's direct supervisor—an assistant principal with authority over teachers, including the power to affect their employment. Under FEHA, an employer is strictly liable for harassment by a supervisor. Gov. Code § 12940(j)(1); see also State Dep't of Health Servs. v. Superior Court (2003) 31 Cal.4th 1026, 1042.

The Bailey Court emphasized that even a coworker's single comment can be actionable, while noting that a "supervisor's words may carry more weight and be more intimidating." Bailey, 16 Cal.5th at 631. When a supervisor publicly demeans an employee, the impact is magnified—the employee reasonably fears that the supervisor's hostility will translate into adverse employment consequences.

*2. The Comments Were Made Publicly in Front of Students*

Mr. Langford did not make his comments privately. He humiliated Dr. Whitcombe in front of her students—the very individuals she needed to respect her authority as their teacher. This public degradation fundamentally undermined Dr. Whitcombe's ability to perform her job.

As the undisputed facts show, after the incident, Dr. Whitcombe's students would not look her in the eye. For the first time in her career, she felt unable to establish authority in her classroom. This is precisely the kind of interference with work performance that FEHA is designed to prevent. See Gov. Code § 12923(a) (harassment must be evaluated by whether "a reasonable person subjected to the discriminatory conduct would find . . . that the harassment so altered working conditions as to make it more difficult to do the job").

*3. The Content of the Comments Was Directly Age-Based and Degrading*

Mr. Langford's comments were not ambiguous. He compared Dr. Whitcombe to a prehistoric creature ("dinosaurs"), mocked her ability to learn ("You can't teach an old dog new tricks"), and directly challenged her professional competence based on her age ("I don't think you're going to be able to figure out the new LMS").

These are classic age-based stereotypes—that older workers are obsolete, incapable of learning, and should be put out to pasture. Such stereotypes are exactly what FEHA prohibits. See Gov. Code § 12940(j)(1) (prohibiting harassment based on age).

*4. The Harm Was Severe and Documented*

The severity of the harassment is demonstrated by its devastating effects on Dr. Whitcombe. She immediately fled the classroom in tears. She developed severe stress and anxiety. She was forced to take leave. And she has been unable to return to work.

Dr. Samuel Pierre, a psychiatrist, has determined that Dr. Whitcombe suffered a permanent disability as a result of the August 26th incident. This documented harm demonstrates that the harassment was not trivial or fleeting—it fundamentally altered the conditions of Dr. Whitcombe's employment and destroyed her ability to perform her job.

**C. Harassment Cases Are Rarely Appropriate for Summary Judgment, and This Case Presents Triable Issues of Fact**

The California Legislature has expressly declared that "[h]arassment cases are rarely appropriate for disposition on summary judgment." Gov. Code § 12923(e). The Legislature recognized that hostile work environment cases "involve issues 'not determinable on paper.'" Id.

This legislative directive exists because determinations about the severity of harassment depend heavily on context, credibility, and the trier of fact's assessment of the circumstances. Whether Mr. Langford's comments were severe enough to create a hostile work environment is precisely the type of factual question that a jury—not a court on summary judgment—should decide.

Here, there are triable issues of fact concerning:

- The severity of Mr. Langford's conduct in context
- The impact of the public humiliation on Dr. Whitcombe's ability to do her job
- Whether a reasonable person would find the conduct created a hostile environment
- The extent of the harm Dr. Whitcombe suffered

KPA may argue that one incident is insufficient as a matter of law, but that argument is foreclosed by Government Code section 12923(b) and Bailey. The question is not whether a single incident *can* be actionable—the Legislature and Supreme Court have confirmed it can—but whether *this* incident was sufficiently severe. That is a factual determination for a jury.

Triable issues of material fact exist regarding whether Mr. Langford's age-based harassment was sufficiently severe to create a hostile work environment under FEHA. This case should proceed to trial.
