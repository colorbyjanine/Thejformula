I. INTRODUCTION

Defendant Kingswell Preparatory Academy ("KPA") moves for summary judgment on Plaintiff Dr. Elaine Whitcombe's claim for harassment based on age in violation of California's Fair Employment and Housing Act ("FEHA"). This motion should be denied.

On August 26, 2024—the very first day of the school year—KPA's Assistant Principal Richard Langford publicly humiliated Dr. Whitcombe in front of her own students by declaring that she "look[ed] like [she had] been around since dinosaurs walked the earth" and that "[y]ou can't teach an old dog new tricks." Two students gasped. No one laughed. Dr. Whitcombe stood frozen in shock before fleeing the classroom in tears. She has never recovered. A psychiatrist has determined that she suffers from a permanent disability as a result of this single, devastating incident.

The California Legislature has expressly declared that "[a] single incident of harassing conduct is sufficient to create a triable issue regarding the existence of a hostile work environment." Cal. Gov. Code § 12923(b). The California Supreme Court has confirmed that "a single incident of harassing conduct may be enough" to state a harassment claim under FEHA. Bailey v. San Francisco Dist. Attorney's Office (2024) 16 Cal.5th 611, 628. And the Legislature has further declared that "[h]arassment cases are rarely appropriate for disposition on summary judgment." Cal. Gov. Code § 12923(e).

Because triable issues of material fact exist as to whether Langford's conduct constituted severe harassment, and because KPA is strictly liable for its supervisor's conduct, defendant's motion for summary judgment must be denied.

II. STATEMENT OF FACTS

Dr. Elaine Whitcombe is a 60-year-old high school English teacher who has dedicated her career to educating students. (Statement of Undisputed Material Facts ["UMF"] ¶ 1.) She was employed at Kingswell Preparatory Academy, a private high school in San Diego employing more than five individuals. (UMF ¶ 2.)

In July 2024, KPA hired Richard Langford as Assistant Principal. (UMF ¶ 3.) Langford had supervisory authority over teachers, including Dr. Whitcombe, and was tasked with overseeing implementation of new teaching technologies. (UMF ¶ 4.)

On August 26, 2024—the first day of the new school year—Langford observed Dr. Whitcombe's class session, sitting in the back of the classroom. (UMF ¶ 5.) During the class, Dr. Whitcombe made a self-deprecating joke to put her students at ease, stating: "I know I may look like I've been around since the dark ages, but trust me, I'm very versed in current technology and will be able to tell if you are plagiarizing." (UMF ¶ 6.) The students laughed warmly. (UMF ¶ 7.)

Dr. Whitcombe continued: "We are going to be using some new learning management system in the classroom. It might take all of us a few weeks to become proficient, but we'll get there together." (UMF ¶ 8.)

At this point, Langford sniggered audibly. He then made the following statement in front of the entire class: "The dark ages? You look more like you've been around since dinosaurs walked the earth. I don't think you're going to be able to figure out the new LMS. You can't teach an old dog new tricks." (UMF ¶ 9.)

No one laughed. Two students gasped. (UMF ¶ 10.)

Dr. Whitcombe stood frozen in shock, humiliated, and unsure what to do. (UMF ¶ 11.) After approximately two minutes of standing in silence, she walked out of her own classroom to the teachers' bathroom and cried. (UMF ¶ 12.) She left work early that day. (UMF ¶ 13.)

The next day, none of her students would look her in the eye—nor the day after. (UMF ¶ 14.) For the first time in her teaching career, Dr. Whitcombe felt she could not establish her authority in the classroom. (UMF ¶ 15.) She began suffering from severe stress and anxiety and had to take leave. (UMF ¶ 16.)

Dr. Samuel Pierre, Plaintiff's expert psychiatrist, determined that Dr. Whitcombe suffered from a permanent disability due to the stress from the August 26th incident. (UMF ¶ 17.) She has yet to be able to return to work. (UMF ¶ 18.)

Prior to filing suit, Dr. Whitcombe obtained the requisite Right to Sue letter from the California Civil Rights Department. (UMF ¶ 19.) Her suit, filed April 21, 2025, included a claim for harassment based on age in violation of FEHA. (UMF ¶ 20.)

III. LEGAL STANDARD

A party moving for summary judgment bears the burden of showing "that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law." Cal. Code Civ. Proc. § 437c(c); Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.

In opposing a motion for summary judgment, the plaintiff need only demonstrate that "a triable issue of one or more material facts exists." Cal. Code Civ. Proc. § 437c(p)(2). Courts must "liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party." State of California v. Allstate Ins. Co. (2009) 45 Cal.4th 1008, 1017-18.

The Legislature has expressly declared that "[h]arassment cases are rarely appropriate for disposition on summary judgment." Cal. Gov. Code § 12923(e). This declaration "affirms the decision in Nazir v. United Airlines, Inc. (2009) 178 Cal.App.4th 243 and the other cited cases." Id.

IV. ARGUMENT

A. A Single Incident of Harassment Can Be Sufficiently Severe to Create a Hostile Work Environment Under FEHA

KPA may argue that because Langford made only a single harassing statement, summary judgment is warranted. This argument fails as a matter of law.

The California Legislature has expressly rejected the notion that multiple incidents are required to establish harassment:

"A single incident of harassing conduct is sufficient to create a triable issue regarding the existence of a hostile work environment if the harassing conduct has unreasonably interfered with the plaintiff's work performance or created an intimidating, hostile, or offensive working environment."

Cal. Gov. Code § 12923(b).

The California Supreme Court confirmed this principle in Bailey v. San Francisco District Attorney's Office (2024) 16 Cal.5th 611. In Bailey, the Court held that "a single incident of harassing conduct may be enough" to constitute actionable harassment under FEHA. Id. at 628. The Court emphasized that "the Legislature intended for the FEHA to provide broader protection than its federal counterpart" and that "California courts should not impose a threshold requirement of multiple incidents." Id. at 626-27.

Here, Langford's conduct satisfies the statutory standard. His public declaration that Dr. Whitcombe looked like she had "been around since dinosaurs walked the earth" and that "[y]ou can't teach an old dog new tricks"—made in front of her own students on the first day of school—unreasonably interfered with her work performance and created an intimidating, hostile, and offensive working environment. The undisputed evidence shows that:

  • Dr. Whitcombe was rendered unable to establish authority in her classroom (UMF ¶ 15);
  • Her students would not look her in the eye after the incident (UMF ¶ 14);
  • She suffered severe stress and anxiety requiring leave (UMF ¶ 16); and
  • She developed a permanent disability and has been unable to return to work (UMF ¶ 17-18).

Under Bailey and Section 12923(b), a single incident this severe is sufficient to create a triable issue of fact.

B. The Totality of the Circumstances Demonstrates That Langford's Conduct Was Severe Enough to Constitute Actionable Harassment

Under FEHA, courts assess harassment claims by examining "the totality of the circumstances." Miller v. Dep't of Corrections (2005) 36 Cal.4th 446, 462; Hughes v. Pair (2009) 46 Cal.4th 1035, 1043. The California Supreme Court has explained that courts must engage in "careful consideration of the social context in which particular behavior occurs and is experienced by its target." Hughes, 46 Cal.4th at 1044.

Multiple factors demonstrate the severity of Langford's conduct:

1. The Conduct Occurred in Front of Dr. Whitcombe's Own Students.

The public nature of the harassment dramatically amplified its severity. Langford did not make these comments privately—he made them in front of an entire classroom of students whom Dr. Whitcombe was tasked with teaching and leading. The students' reaction—two gasped, no one laughed—demonstrates the shocking nature of his statements. See Hughes, 46 Cal.4th at 1044 (courts must consider "the social context in which particular behavior occurs").

2. The Harasser Was Dr. Whitcombe's Direct Supervisor.

Langford was not a coworker making an offhand comment. He was the Assistant Principal with direct supervisory authority over Dr. Whitcombe. His public undermining of her competence in front of students carried the implicit weight of his authority. When a supervisor publicly declares that a teacher "can't teach an old dog new tricks," students receive a clear message about that teacher's capabilities—and the teacher's ability to command respect is permanently damaged.

3. The Comments Were Explicitly Age-Based.

Langford's statements were unmistakably tied to Dr. Whitcombe's age. Comparing her to "dinosaurs" and stating she "can't teach an old dog new tricks" are quintessential age-based slurs that invoke stereotypes about older workers being technologically incompetent and incapable of learning. These comments went directly to the core of her professional competence.

4. The Conduct Caused Permanent, Disabling Harm.

The consequences of Langford's conduct were catastrophic. Dr. Whitcombe has been diagnosed with a permanent disability. She has been unable to return to work. A single incident caused permanent psychological harm—compelling evidence of its severity.

5. The Timing Maximized Damage.

The harassment occurred on the first day of school, when Dr. Whitcombe was establishing her relationship with new students. Langford's public attack destroyed her ability to establish authority at the most critical moment of the school year.

Viewed in totality, these circumstances demonstrate that Langford's conduct was sufficiently severe to constitute harassment. At minimum, they create triable issues of material fact that preclude summary judgment.

C. KPA Is Strictly Liable for Supervisor Langford's Harassment

Even if KPA argues it did not know of or condone Langford's conduct, such a defense fails as a matter of law. Under FEHA, an employer is strictly liable for harassment committed by a supervisor.

California Government Code section 12940(j)(1) provides that an employer is liable for harassment when the harasser is "the employer's . . . supervisors." The California Supreme Court has held that "under the FEHA, an employer is strictly liable for all acts of sexual harassment by a supervisor." State Dep't of Health Servs. v. Superior Court (2003) 31 Cal.4th 1026, 1042. This rule applies equally to harassment based on age or any other protected characteristic. See id.

The rationale for strict liability is clear: "supervisors act for the employer because of their authority over hiring, firing, and working conditions, and because supervisors have the power to make the employee feel powerless to resist harassment." Id. at 1040-41.

Here, the undisputed facts establish that Langford was Dr. Whitcombe's supervisor. He held the position of Assistant Principal with "supervisory authority over teachers, including Dr. Whitcombe." (UMF ¶ 4.) His harassment occurred in the course of his supervisory duties—observing and commenting on Dr. Whitcombe's classroom performance.

Accordingly, KPA is strictly liable for Langford's harassment. No defense based on lack of knowledge or corrective action can shield KPA from liability.

D. Summary Judgment Is Rarely Appropriate in Harassment Cases, and Triable Issues of Fact Preclude Summary Judgment Here

The California Legislature has declared that "[h]arassment cases are rarely appropriate for disposition on summary judgment." Cal. Gov. Code § 12923(e). In enacting this provision, the Legislature expressly "affirms the decision in Nazir v. United Airlines, Inc. (2009) 178 Cal.App.4th 243." Id.

In Nazir, the Court of Appeal explained why summary judgment is disfavored in harassment cases: determining whether conduct is "severe or pervasive" enough to create a hostile work environment is "not a question that lends itself to summary adjudication" because it "require[s] assessment of the credibility of testimony and the weighing of disputed facts." 178 Cal.App.4th at 286.

This case exemplifies why harassment claims should proceed to trial. The severity of Langford's conduct, the impact on Dr. Whitcombe, and whether a reasonable person would find the work environment hostile are quintessential jury questions. Credibility determinations—such as whether Dr. Whitcombe's reaction was reasonable or whether Langford's conduct was as egregious as described—cannot be resolved on summary judgment.

Under the summary judgment standard set forth in Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, all reasonable inferences must be drawn in favor of the non-moving party. Here, the evidence creates—at minimum—triable issues as to:

  1. Whether Langford's conduct was sufficiently severe to constitute harassment;
  2. Whether the conduct unreasonably interfered with Dr. Whitcombe's work performance;
  3. Whether the conduct created a hostile or offensive work environment; and
  4. The extent of damages Dr. Whitcombe suffered.

Where triable issues of material fact exist, summary judgment must be denied. Id. at 843.

V. CONCLUSION

The undisputed facts establish that KPA's supervisor publicly humiliated Dr. Whitcombe in front of her own students with explicitly age-based slurs, rendering her unable to perform her job and causing permanent disability. The California Legislature has declared that a single incident of harassment can be sufficient and that harassment cases rarely belong on summary judgment. The California Supreme Court has confirmed these principles. KPA is strictly liable for its supervisor's conduct.

For all of the foregoing reasons, Plaintiff Dr. Elaine Whitcombe respectfully requests that this Court deny Defendant's Motion for Summary Judgment in its entirety.
